How icons are made small

Short answer: designers do zoom in, but in two different senses. First, they work zoomed in on the screen, at 800 to 1600 %, on the actual 16 × 16 or 32 × 32 grid of pixels, and redraw that size by hand. Shrinking the big picture is not enough. Second, the drawing itself is sometimes zoomed: it is cropped to the part people recognise. MS2 does that. The other common move is to keep the whole picture and take detail out: fewer lines, a bigger centre, heavier strokes. The small M5c (MC) does that. Every small size is then checked at 100 % in the places it really shows.

Why not simply scale down? A 16 px icon has 256 pixels. Scaled from 1024, every line of M5c becomes thinner than a pixel and turns into grey haze, the fine field lines vanish, and the nucleus shrinks to a smudge. Apple says details "may be hard to discern at smaller sizes" and warns against very thin lines. Microsoft says to "maintain legibility at small sizes". Here is the same M5c both ways:

    Apple's current guidance asks for one 1024 picture and lets the system scale it; macOS 26's Icon Composer file has no slot for a hand-drawn 16. Hand-drawn small sizes still go in through the classic route: the .icns file (16 and 32 at 1x and 2x) that AudioLab already ships, and the .ico for Windows. Whether macOS 26 prefers the .icns slots over the Icon Composer file in the Finder list is not verified; the implementation task checks it on a Mac.
